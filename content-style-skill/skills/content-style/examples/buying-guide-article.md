# How to Choose a Standing Desk

A standing desk deserves careful choosing for one quiet reason: the failure mode is invisible in a store listing. A frame that wobbles at full extension reveals itself weeks later, on a video call, with a coffee sitting on it — and no spec sheet prints "wobbles at your height." The factors below are ranked roughly by how much they decide long-term satisfaction, with price deliberately last, because a cheap unstable desk costs more in frustration than it saves in dollars.

## What Matters

- **Stability at your full standing height.** The single most common regret. Every frame gains wobble as it rises; the differences between frames are how much, and wide feet and heavier-gauge steel typically fare better at full extension than minimal frames. Owner reviews of the specific frame, where available, are the honest source here — and worth reading before buying.
- **Height range that fits your body.** The desk must reach both your seated and standing elbow heights. Most electric frames adjust somewhere in the 25-to-50-inch range, which covers typical users but not everyone: measure before shopping, using the method below in "Fit Check."
- **Surface size for your real work.** Monitor count, keyboard and mouse footprint, and anything that lives on the desk permanently (speakers, a lamp, a document tray). Tops commonly run 48 to 60 inches wide; below 48, dual monitors get tight.
- **Mechanism.** Electric with memory presets, manual crank, or a converter that sits on your existing desk. Decided in "Your Options."
- **Weight capacity.** A spec-sheet figure — take your setup's weight and leave headroom, especially if a monitor arm hangs off the back edge.
- **Cable management.** Grommets and an under-top tray keep the desk's one moving part — the surface — from dragging your cables along with it.

## Your Options

- **Electric standing desk** (frame plus a separate top): motorized height adjustment, memory presets for two or more stored heights, the choice for daily sit-stand switching.
- **Manual crank desk**: no motor, nothing electrical to fail, typically cheaper, and slow enough that height changes become occasional rather than constant.
- **Desk converter**: a riser that sits on your existing desk and lifts just the keyboard and monitor tier. No uninstall, no commitment — the low-cost way to test whether standing helps you at all.

## Comparing the Options

| Decision factor | Electric desk | Crank desk | Desk converter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adjustment speed | Seconds, one button | Roughly half a minute of cranking | Manual lift or crank; varies by model |
| Typical price band (varies by region and sales) | Commonly around $250–$600+ for well-regarded frames | Typically under $300 | Typically $60–$250 |
| Stability | Good to very good; varies most by frame | Typically solid; no motor housing to shift | Depends on your existing desk's stability |
| Best fit | Daily up-and-down switching | Occasional height changes on a budget | Testing standing before committing hardware |

## Which Situation Are You In?

- **Switching posture several times a day:** electric with presets. The switching is the benefit; anything that adds friction to it removes the point.
- **Renting, or not ready to commit:** a converter. It leaves with you, and it answers the "would I actually stand?" question for the price of a nice dinner.
- **On a strict budget:** crank. You lose speed, not stability, and motors are the expensive half anyway.
- **Taller or shorter than typical:** check the height range before anything else — a well-reviewed frame that stops short of your standing elbow height is the wrong desk at any price.
- **Sitting for fewer than a couple of hours at a stretch:** honestly, possibly none of these. A walk between tasks costs nothing and needs no assembly.

## Fit Check and the Final Checklist

Measure both elbow heights before you compare anything: sit at your working chair and drop your forearms flat; measure floor to elbow. Then stand relaxed, bend one elbow to 90 degrees, and measure floor to that elbow. Both numbers — plus an inch of margin on each — are what the frame's range must cover.

Before buying, confirm:

- The frame's height range covers both your elbow-height numbers with margin.
- The top's width fits your monitors and your room, with depth enough for the keyboard at elbow distance.
- Weight capacity exceeds your setup's weight with headroom for later additions.
- Owner reports on the specific frame mention full-height stability, not just assembly ease.
- You know your honest answer to the crank-versus-motor question: will you adjust a desk by hand twice a day?

Get those five right and the desk stops being furniture you negotiate with. The rest — top finish, controller style — is preference.
