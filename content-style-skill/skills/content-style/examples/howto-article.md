# How to Stop a Running Toilet

The trickle you hear after the tank refills — the sound of a toilet that never quite stops running — almost always comes from one of two parts at the top of the tank wearing out of adjustment: the flapper or the fill valve. The fix for either typically takes about 15 minutes and no plumbing experience, and both replacement parts are inexpensive enough that replacing a suspect on suspicion is a reasonable plan. Diagnose first, though; the two tests below tell you which part is actually failing before you buy anything.

## What You'll Need

- A few drops of food coloring
- A sponge and a small towel
- A replacement flapper (only if the leak test below points there — bring the old one to the store or photograph its mounting ears)
- If the fill valve is the culprit: a replacement fill valve kit, a bucket, and an adjustable wrench

## Find the Problem, Then Fix It

1. Lift the tank lid off and set it flat on a towel beside the toilet.
2. Look at the overflow tube — the open vertical pipe in the middle of the tank. If the water level sits at or above its rim and runs into it, the fill valve side is the problem; continue at the fill valve steps below.
3. Drop enough food coloring into the tank water to tint it noticeably.
4. Wait 15 to 20 minutes without flushing.
5. Check the bowl. Color appearing in the bowl means the flapper is leaking tank water into it.
6. Lift the flapper and feel its underside and its seat for mineral buildup or warping.
7. Wipe the flapper's seat clean with the sponge if it feels gritty.
8. Inspect the chain between the flush handle and the flapper. It should have about half an inch of slack — a chain pulled tight holds the flapper open a crack; a chain with a kink in it can do the same.
9. Replace the flapper if it looks warped, cracked, or stiff: shut the supply valve, flush to drain the tank, unhook the old flapper from its mounting ears, and hook the replacement on the same ears with the same chain slack.
10. Turn the supply valve back on and let the tank refill.
11. Flush once and watch the flapper through the cycle. It must lift fully when the handle drops and settle squarely back on its seat when the tank drains.

## If the Fill Valve Is the Problem

> **Warning:** Shut the supply valve — the small handle on the wall or floor behind the toilet — fully clockwise before unscrewing anything below the tank's water line, and keep a bucket under the supply nut. An open connection with the supply on will spray household-pressure water.

1. Check the water level after the tank refills and stops. It should sit about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.
2. Lower the level if it sits higher: on older valves, bend the brass float arm gently downward; on newer cup-style floats, twist the adjustment clip or screw per the valve's markings.
3. Flush and let the tank refill to confirm the level and that the valve stops running.
4. Replace the valve if it keeps running past the adjusted level: shut the supply, drain the tank by flushing and sponging, unscrew the supply nut under the tank with the wrench, lift the old valve out, and drop the new one in following the kit's instructions, hand-tightening the mounting nut.

## Troubleshooting

- Short bursts of running every few minutes (a "phantom flush"): a small flapper leak — rerun the coloring test; a replaced flapper that still leaks points to a nicked seat, which a flush-valve replacement kit or a plumber addresses.
- Constant trickle with the water level below the overflow rim: the flapper is not seating — reseat it and check the chain slack first.
- Hissing from the fill valve long after refill: the supply valve below the tank may be partly closed, or the valve itself is worn; open the supply fully, and plan on replacing the valve if the hiss continues.
- Water on the floor around the toilet's base: different problem — that points below the tank, at the supply connection, the tank bolts, or the wax ring, and it is where most people should call a plumber.

## When to Call a Plumber

Every fix on this page happens at the top of the tank, where the water is clean and the pressure is gravity alone. The line is drawn at corroded tank threads, a cracked flush valve seat, a supply valve that will not fully close, or any leak at the bowl's base — those involve pressurized connections or pulling the toilet, and a wrong turn there costs more than the service call. Knowing that boundary is part of the repair.
