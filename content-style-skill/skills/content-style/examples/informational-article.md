# Why Houseplant Leaves Turn Yellow (and How to Fix It)

Yellow leaves usually mean the roots are stressed, and overwatering is the most common cause by a wide margin. Before doing anything else, press a finger two inches into the soil: if it comes out damp, the problem is water — and the fix is a longer gap between waterings, not a smaller drink each time.

The yellowing itself is not a disease. It is a symptom — botanically, chlorosis, the leaf losing chlorophyll — with a short list of realistic causes, and the pattern usually identifies which one you are facing: which leaves yellow, how fast, and what the soil feels like.

## Common Causes

### Overwatering (the usual suspect)

Lower and inner leaves yellow first, soften slightly, and stay yellow; the soil stays wet longer than a few days, and fungus gnats sometimes appear as a companion symptom. Roots kept wet suffocate and rot, and a rotted root system cannot supply the top growth.

Fix: check the soil before every watering and water only when the top two inches are dry. Make sure the pot drains freely and empties into a saucer you actually empty. If the soil smells sour or the plant is clearly waterlogged, repot into fresh mix and trim any soft brown roots while you are in there.

### Underwatering

Leaves yellow and turn dry or crispy at the edges, often all over the plant at once, and the soil pulls away from the inside of the pot. Underwatered roots die back just like flooded ones, just by the opposite route.

Fix: water thoroughly, until it runs from the drainage holes, and shorten the interval. A pot that dries out in two days needs either more frequent water or a larger soil volume — small pots dry fast.

### Too little light

Yellowing spreads pale and general, growth slows or stops, and new stems come in long and thin reaching for a window. Low light starves the leaf-making process the leaves exist to run.

Fix: move the plant gradually to a brighter position — a sudden jump scorches leaves that grew for dim conditions. A spot near (not necessarily in) an east- or west-facing window is the usual upgrade.

### Natural aging

The lowest leaves yellow one at a time, slowly, while the rest of the plant keeps producing healthy new growth. Old leaves are retired, not lost.

Fix: none needed. Once a leaf is fully yellow it will not green again; remove it cleanly at the base of its stalk.

### Nutrient deficiency

The pattern varies with the nutrient, but a common version is steady, general paling over weeks in a plant that has been in the same pot for a year or more, with the soil clearly exhausted. Deficiency is a plausible diagnosis mainly for pot-bound, long-unfed plants — it is much rarer than the causes above.

Fix: feed at half the label's strength with a balanced houseplant fertilizer during the growing season, and consider repotting into fresh mix instead of feeding harder. Fertilizer is a late move in this diagnosis, not a first response — feeding a plant whose roots are waterlogged makes things worse.

### Pests

Yellowing accompanied by stippling — tiny pale pinpricks — webbing at leaf joints, or visible insects on leaf undersides points to spider mites, thrips, or their relatives rather than to care.

Fix: inspect the undersides of leaves, rinse the plant thoroughly with lukewarm water, and follow with insecticidal soap applied to all leaf surfaces, repeated per the label's interval. Isolate the plant from its neighbors until the infestation clears.

## An Order That Works

Diagnose cheapest-first: the soil finger test, then any recent change (a move, a repot, the season shifting), then the light, then a magnified look at the leaf undersides. Only a plant that passes all four checks should get fertilizer. Most yellowing resolves before the bottom of that list, which is the point of the order.

## Related Questions

### Should I cut yellow leaves off?

Once a leaf is fully yellow, remove it — it will not recover, and the plant is done reclaiming it. A partly yellow leaf with green tissue is still producing energy; leave it until the green is gone, then cut cleanly at the stalk's base.

### Will yellow leaves turn green again?

Usually no. Partially yellow leaves sometimes recover if the cause is fixed early — watch for the green areas holding firm rather than expanding yellow. New growth coming in green is the real signal the fix worked.

### How long until I see improvement?

Give the plant a few weeks after any change before judging it. Growth responses are slow, and new leaves — the evidence that matters — take time to emerge; how long varies by plant and season, and tends to run faster in spring and summer than in a dim winter.

Most yellow leaves trace back to the first check on the list: damp soil two inches down. Start there, fix the interval, and give the plant a few weeks before concluding anything worse is happening.
