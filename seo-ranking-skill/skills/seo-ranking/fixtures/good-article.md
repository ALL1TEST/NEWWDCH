# How to Store Fresh Basil So It Lasts for Weeks

Fresh basil is the most perishable herb in a standard shopping basket, and most bunches have turned to slime before the weekend pasta sauce comes around. Knowing how to store fresh basil well is less about fancy equipment and more about matching the method to how quickly you cook: fresh basil lasts 5–7 days at room temperature in a jar of water, 7–10 days wrapped in a damp paper towel in the refrigerator, and months when frozen. Below, each method in detail, plus the mistakes that quietly ruin an otherwise healthy bunch. Basil (Ocimum basilicum) is a warm-climate herb, and nearly every storage mistake in this article comes down to treating it like a cold-climate one.

## Storing Fresh Basil at Room Temperature

For a bunch you will realistically use within the week, the bouquet method keeps basil happiest, because a cut bunch is still a living plant that drinks through its stems. Trim the last centimetre off the stem ends under running water — the same trick you use to refresh cut flowers — and stand the bunch in a glass of cool water. Keep the jar on the counter away from direct sunlight; a south-facing windowsill will scorch the leaves rather than light them. Change the water whenever it starts to cloud, usually every day or two, and pull off any leaves that yellow early, because one softening leaf speeds up its neighbours.

If your kitchen air is very dry in winter, loop a loose, open plastic bag over the top of the jar to slow moisture loss from the leaves — a closed bag traps exactly the humidity that turns basil slimy. Treated this way, a healthy bunch stays perky and aromatic for roughly 5–7 days, and the growing tip often keeps producing new leaves, which is the clearest sign the plant is still content. A jar that still smells sweet on day five is doing its job.

## Keeping Basil Fresh in the Refrigerator

When the week gets away from you, the refrigerator buys extra time — but only if you keep the leaves dry and the cold gentle. Pick the leaves off the stems and wash nothing at this stage; moisture on stored leaves is the fastest route to slime. Wrap the dry leaves loosely in a paper towel that you have dampened lightly — it should feel barely humid, not wet — then slide the bundle into a bag left partly open and place it in the mildest part of the fridge, such as the door shelf rather than the coldest drawer at the bottom.

Stored this way, basil holds its aroma for about 7–10 days, which is usually enough to bridge a late-week cooking plan. The paper towel does the real work: it buffers humidity so the leaves neither dry out nor sit in condensation. Check it every couple of days and swap in a fresh towel when the old one smells sharp. Whole stems stored this way also survive, but picked leaves pack flatter and take less fridge space for the same amount of basil.

## Freezing Fresh Basil

Freezing is the long-term answer when a bunch has outlasted every plan for it: freeze the leaves, either whole or chopped and mixed with a splash of olive oil to protect their aroma, and use them straight from the freezer in cooked dishes within a few months.

## Why Does Basil Turn Black in the Refrigerator?

Blackened, translucent patches on refrigerated basil are chilling injury, not mould and not spoilage. Basil is a tropical plant, and sustained cold ruptures its leaf cells; the damaged tissue then oxidises dark, the same way a cut apple browns at room temperature. The damage shows up first in the coldest zones of the fridge and in bags pressed flat against the back wall, which is why the mild-spot placement above matters. Leaves with small trimmed-away patches are still safe to eat, but the aroma goes flat, so treat blackening as the signal to use the bunch immediately in something cooked.

## Should You Wash Basil Before Storing It?

Wash basil only immediately before you use it. Wet leaves bruise wherever they touch each other, and residual water left on leaves in a bag or jar accelerates decay within a day or two. If a bunch arrives genuinely muddy, rinse it, then dry the leaves thoroughly — a salad spinner followed by a few minutes on a clean towel — before committing them to any storage method. Both the counter jar and the refrigerator wrap described above assume dry leaves; washing is the last step before cooking, not the first step of storage.

## Common Mistakes That Shorten Basil's Life

Most lost bunches trace back to the same four habits. Storing loose leaves in the coldest drawer of the fridge guarantees chilling injury within days. Sealing leaves in a fully closed bag, whether they are wet or dry, traps moisture and turns them slimy. Leaving a bunch on the counter out of water treats a living plant like a garnish, and it will be limp by the next morning. And refrigerating the water jar combines every failure mode at once — cold, condensation, and still water — which is why the two methods above are listed separately.

Two softer habits are worth noticing too. If you keep losing stored bunches anyway, [growing basil indoors](/growing-basil-indoors) on a bright windowsill gives you cut-and-come-again leaves with no storage math at all. And when a bunch is one day from the bin, [basil pesto](/basil-pesto-recipe) is the use-it-now answer that beats every storage method — leaves too limp for a salad still blend down perfectly well. Beyond that point, [drying herbs](/how-to-dry-herbs) is the salvage path: a bunch past its prime for anything fresh still dries down into a decent jar of basil for winter soups.
